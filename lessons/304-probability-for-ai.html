Under the hood: where the chances come from optional

The model gives every token in its vocabulary a raw score (tens of thousands of tokens or more, see course 103). A formula called softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%:

chance of a word = escore ÷ T ÷ (sum of escore ÷ T for every word)

T is the temperature. Dividing by a small T stretches the gaps between scores, so the favorite wins more. A big T shrinks the gaps, so the bars even out. Many tools also offer top-p or top-k, which throw away the unlikely tail before the pick.

This toy uses whole words to stay readable. Real models pick tokens, which are often pieces of words.

What just happened

The bars are a probability distribution: every possible next word with its chance. The chances always add up to 100%. Picking one at random, following those chances, is called sampling. Likely words win most of the time, but unlikely ones still win sometimes. That's why the same prompt can give different answers.

When you asked 100 times, the share of picks landed close to the bars, but not exactly on them. Chance is lumpy in small numbers and smooth in large ones. Hold on to that idea: it's the whole of part 2.

Temperature reshapes the chances before the pick. Low temperature makes the favorite even more likely. At 0 the AI always takes the top word. High temperature flattens the bars, so odd words like "purple" get a real shot.

TemperatureWhat you getGood for
0 to 0.3Nearly the same answer every timePulling data out of text, sorting into categories, code
0.5 to 1Varied but sensibleWriting, brainstorming, chat
Above 1.2Surprising, often nonsenseRarely useful

When you build with AI: most AI APIs have a temperature setting. The allowed range depends on the provider, often 0 to 1 or 0 to 2, and some newer models fix it for you. Even at 0, answers can occasionally differ slightly, so don't build a feature that breaks if the wording changes.

Why small tests fool you

Every test is a sample, and samples wobble. A 20-message test of an AI that's truly 85% accurate can easily score 70% or 100%. The average of many tests sits near the truth, but the spread tells you how far any single test can wander.

Bigger samples wobble less. As tests grow, their scores crowd in around the true value. Mathematicians call this the law of large numbers. It's why a single impressive demo proves very little.

Messages in the testWhere 95% of scores land (AI truly 85% accurate)
20about 70% to 100%
200about 80% to 90%
1,000about 83% to 87%
Under the hood: the square-root rule optional

For a yes/no score, the typical wobble (the standard error) is about √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n), where p is the true rate and n is the number of examples. About 95% of tests land within two of those wobbles of the truth.

Because of the square root, halving the wobble takes four times as many examples. Going from 20 to 80 messages helps a lot. Going from 2,000 to 8,000 helps much less.

This assumes each example is independent and like the ones you'll see for real. If all 20 test messages are neat and polite, a bigger test of neat messages still won't tell you how the AI copes with messy ones.

This is the base rate problem. The base rate is how common something is before you test for it. When the thing you're hunting is rarer than the checker's mistakes, the honest cases are so numerous that their few errors outnumber the real finds. Spam filters, fraud checks, "written by AI" detectors and security scanners all live with this.

Under the hood: Bayes' rule optional

The grid is a picture of Bayes' rule. The chance that a flagged order is fraud = (fraud orders that get flagged) ÷ (all orders that get flagged).

With 1 in 100 fraud and a 99% accurate checker: out of 1,000 orders, 10 are fraud and about 10 of those are flagged. Of the 990 honest orders, 1% (about 10) are flagged by mistake. So 10 ÷ 20 = 50%.

Counting in real numbers of cases like this ("10 out of 1,000") is the most reliable way to avoid the trap. Percentages of percentages confuse almost everyone, including doctors in studies.

    Under the hood: how computers make "random" numbers optional

    Math.random() isn't truly random. It runs a formula that produces numbers that look random and are spread evenly between 0 and 1. That's called a pseudo-random number generator, and it's perfect for simulations.

    It's not safe for passwords or secret keys, because a clever attacker could predict it. For those, code uses the browser's crypto.getRandomValues().

    Simulations like this one are called Monte Carlo methods, after the casino. When a probability question is hard to solve on paper, you can simply try it thousands of times and count.

    Five quick questions. Each answer explains itself.

    Key terms

    The words you'll meet when people talk about AI, chance and testing.

    Probability
    How likely something is, from 0% (never) to 100% (always).
    Distribution
    Every possible outcome with its chance, like the bars for the next word. The chances add up to 100%.
    Sampling
    Picking an outcome at random, following the chances. It's how an AI chooses each token.
    Temperature
    A setting that sharpens (low) or flattens (high) the chances before sampling. Use low values for extraction and code.
    Law of large numbers
    The more samples you take, the closer their average gets to the true value.
    Average and spread
    The average is the typical result. The spread is how far single results wander from it. You need both.
    Evaluation (eval)
    Testing an AI feature on many realistic examples and counting how often it's right. Small evals wobble a lot.
    Base rate
    How common something is before you test. When it's rare, even accurate checkers raise mostly false alarms.
    False positive
    A flag or "yes" that turns out to be wrong, like an honest order marked as fraud.
    Simulation
    Using random numbers to try something thousands of times and count what happens.

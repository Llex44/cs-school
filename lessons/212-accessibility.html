    What just happened

    Every barrier you found is invisible to someone with sharp eyes, a mouse and fast Wi-Fi. That's also how an AI checks its own work: it looks at the code or a screenshot, not at a person trying to pay. So AI-built pages often look finished and still lock people out.

    Screen readers don't see pixels at all. The browser turns your HTML into an accessibility tree: a list of things with a role (button, link, heading, image), a name (the words that describe it) and a state (checked, invalid, expanded). A styled div has no role. An icon has no name. That's why the page you heard was full of "button, button".

    Under the hood: the accessibility tree optional

    For <button aria-label="Open basket"> the browser builds a node like role: button, name: "Open basket". Screen readers, voice control ("click Open basket") and switch devices all read that tree, not the screen.

    Real HTML elements come with the right role and keyboard behavior for free: <button>, <a href>, <label>, <h1>, <input>. ARIA attributes like aria-label fill gaps, but the first rule of ARIA is: if a real element does the job, use it.

    You can see the tree yourself: in Chrome, open DevTools, pick an element and open the Accessibility tab.

    One fix, many people

    Designers call this the persona spectrum: a limit can be permanent, temporary, or just the situation you're in right now. Pick a fix.

    The curb-cut effect

    Sloped curb cuts were added to sidewalks for wheelchair users. Today they're used all day by strollers, suitcases, bikes and delivery carts. Captions, keyboard shortcuts, dark mode and voice control all started the same way.

    So when an AI says accessibility is "for a small group of users", it's wrong twice: the group isn't small, and the fixes help everyone. For the visual side of contrast, spacing and type, see Course 211.

    Under the hood: WCAG and the contrast math optional

    WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the standard most laws and companies point to. Level AA is the usual target. It sets rules like 4.5 : 1 contrast for body text, 3 : 1 for large text and for icons and input borders, and a minimum touch target of 24 by 24 pixels. Apple and Google recommend 44 to 48 for touch.

    The ratio compares the relative luminance (perceived brightness) of two colors: (lighter + 0.05) ÷ (darker + 0.05). White on black is 21 : 1, the maximum. Identical colors are 1 : 1.

    When you ask an AI for "accessible" design, ask for "WCAG 2.2 AA" by name. It's specific and testable.

Neural networks learn by being a little less wrong.

Nobody writes the rules. A network starts with random numbers, makes guesses, measures how wrong they were, and nudges every number downhill. You'll tune a neuron by hand, roll a ball down an error landscape, catch a model memorizing, and write the training loop yourself.

Inside the neuron

Under the hood: the neuron's formula optional

The neuron works out a score, then squashes it into a chance:

score = w₁ × softness + w₂ × yellowness + bias chance ripe = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score)

The S-shaped squash is called the sigmoid. It's one of several activation functions. Modern networks mostly use even simpler ones inside, like ReLU, which just turns negative scores into 0. Without some squash or bend like this, stacking neurons would still only ever draw straight lines.

Despite the name, an artificial neuron is only loosely inspired by brain cells. It's a weighted sum and a squash, nothing more.

What just happened

Your neuron did three small things. It multiplied each input by its weight, added the results together with the bias to get a score, and squashed the score into a chance between 0% and 100%. A score above 0 means a chance over 50%, so the neuron says "ripe".

The weights say how much each input counts, and in which direction. A big softness weight means softness matters a lot. A negative weight would mean "more of this makes ripe less likely". The bias sets how easily the neuron says yes before it looks at anything.

The line where the score is exactly 0 is the decision boundary. One neuron can only draw a straight line. Networks stack thousands of neurons in layers, each feeding the next, so their boundaries can bend and twist. That's what lets them recognize faces or read handwriting.

And "Let it learn" found good dials without you. That's training: start with any numbers, measure how wrong they are, nudge them to be less wrong, and repeat. One full pass through all the examples is an epoch. Part 2 shows exactly how the nudging works.

When you build with AI: everything a model "knows" is stored as numbers like these weights, billions of them, set during training. It has no list of facts to look things up in. That's why it can sound certain and still be wrong: it produces what its weights make likely, not what it has checked. To ground it in real facts, hand it your documents, as in course 305. (Is color really a poor guide to ripeness? For many mango varieties, yes: some stay green when ripe, so a gentle squeeze tells you more. Our 20 mangos are made up to show this.)

Learning is rolling downhill

"Let it learn" wasn't magic. It measured how wrong the neuron was, worked out which way to turn each dial to be less wrong, and turned them a little. To see that clearly, here's a model with only one dial. The stand wants to guess how many days until a mango is ripe from how firm it is: days = w × firmness. What's the best w?

Under the hood: the slope, worked out optional

For these six mangos the loss is the average of (w × firmness − days)². Worked out, that's a U-shaped curve:

loss(w) = 38.7 w² − 55 w + 19.7 slope(w) = 77.3 w − 55

The bottom is where the slope is 0: w ≈ 0.71, or about 0.7 days per point of firmness. Each step moves w by learning rate × slope. With a rate above about 0.026, every step overshoots the bottom by more than the last, which is why the ball flew off.

What just happened

To train anything, you need one number that says how wrong the model is: the loss. Here it's the average squared error. For each mango, take the guess minus the real number of days, square it, and average them all. Squaring makes every miss count as positive, and makes big misses count extra.

Try every possible w, plot its loss, and you get a landscape. Training means finding the lowest point. But the model can't see the whole landscape. It only knows the slope right under the ball, called the gradient. So it steps downhill: new w = w − learning rate × slope. That's gradient descent. Where the ground is steep it takes big steps, and near the bottom the steps shrink by themselves.

Learning rateWhat happensIn real training
Too smallCreeps downhill, step after stepWastes hours of expensive computer time
About rightReaches the bottom in a few stepsThe loss falls quickly, then levels off
Too bigOvershoots, bounces, can blow upThe loss jumps around or becomes NaN ("not a number")

Your neuron in Part 1 did the same thing with three dials at once. Its landscape had three directions, and the gradient said which way was downhill in each one. A language model does it with billions of dials. Nobody can draw a landscape with a billion directions, but the slope can still be calculated for every dial. The method is called backpropagation, because it works backward from the loss through each layer.

A straight line is the stiffest curve there is. Slide the flexibility up and watch the curve bend toward the mangos.

The training error says how well the curve fits mangos it has already seen. The test error says how well it does on new ones, which is the only thing your users will ever experience. At full flexibility the curve threads every training mango exactly, then swings wildly in between. It memorized the random wobble in those 8 mangos instead of the real trend. That's overfitting. The opposite, a curve too stiff to follow the real pattern, is underfitting.

The fix starts with a habit: before training, set some examples aside as a test set, often 10 to 20% of the data. Never train on them and never tune on them. Their score is your honest estimate of how the model will do for real.

When you build with AI: the same trap exists for prompts. If you keep tweaking a prompt until it aces your 10 favorite examples, you've fit the prompt to those examples. Keep a separate set of test cases you don't tune on, and make it big enough to trust (course 304 shows why small tests wobble).

    Under the hood: real training loops optional

    Real projects use libraries such as PyTorch or JAX. You describe the model, and the library works out every slope for you automatically, using backpropagation.

    Instead of all the examples at once, real loops usually take a small random batch for each step, called stochastic gradient descent. And instead of one fixed learning rate, most use smarter step rules like Adam, which adjusts the step size for each weight as it goes.

    The shape of the loop is the same as yours: guess, measure the loss, find the slopes, step downhill, repeat.

    From your loop to a language model

    You just wrote the loop that trains every neural network. A large language model, the kind behind AI coding tools (course 103), is trained with the same loop. What changes is the scale.

    The bars use a squeezed scale: each extra stretch of bar means 10 times more numbers. On a normal scale, GPT-3's bar would be about 58 billion times longer than your neuron's. Many newer models don't publish their size at all.

    1. Take real text. Trillions of tokens of books, websites and code. (Tokens are words or pieces of words.)
    2. Hide the next token and guess it. The model gives every possible token a chance, like the bars in course 304.
    3. Measure the surprise. The loss is low if the model gave the real next token a high chance, and high if it was caught off guard.
    4. Nudge every weight downhill. Backpropagation finds the slope for each of the billions of weights, and each moves a tiny step. Then on to the next token.

    Train on one sentence

    The training text says "Ripe mangos smell sweet." The model has to guess the last word.

    Ripe mangos smell ?

    This toy jumps a lot in one step so you can see it move. In a real model a single step barely changes anything. It's hundreds of billions of steps, over trillions of tokens, that add up. After this pretraining, the model is trained further on examples of good conversations and on human feedback about which answers are better. That's what turns a next-token predictor into a helpful assistant.

    When you build with AI: the weights are frozen when you use a model. It doesn't learn from your chats. Anything it seems to remember is text sent along with your message (course 103). To teach it your facts, put them in the prompt or retrieve them. Training a model yourself, or fine-tuning one, is usually the last thing to try, not the first.

    Check your understanding

    Five quick questions. Each answer explains itself.

    Key terms

    The words you'll hear when people talk about how AI models are trained.

    Neuron
    A small unit that multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and squashes the result.
    Weight
    A number that sets how much one input counts, and in which direction. Training adjusts the weights.
    Bias
    A number added to a neuron's score. It sets how easily the neuron says yes.
    Parameters
    All the numbers a model learns, its weights and biases. "175 billion parameters" counts these.
    Activation function
    The squash or bend applied to a neuron's score, like the S-shaped sigmoid. It lets stacked layers learn curved patterns.
    Decision boundary
    The line or surface where a model switches from one answer to the other.
    Loss
    One number for how wrong the model's guesses are. Training tries to push it down.
    Gradient
    The slope of the loss for each weight: which way is uphill, and how steep it is.
    Gradient descent
    Moving every weight a small step downhill, over and over: weight = weight − learning rate × gradient.
    Learning rate
    How big each step is. Too small is slow. Too big overshoots and can blow up.
    Epoch
    One full pass through all the training examples.
    Overfitting
    Fitting the training examples so closely, random noise included, that the model does worse on new data.
    Test set
    Examples held back from training, used only to measure how the model does on data it hasn't seen.
    Backpropagation
    The method that works out the gradient for every weight in a network, working backward from the loss.

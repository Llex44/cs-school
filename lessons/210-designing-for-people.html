Core curriculum · Course 210 · Designing for people

Design starts with a person, not a screen.

Before anything is drawn, someone decides who an app is for, what they are trying to get done, and which steps stand in their way. You'll meet three neighbors who share one garden app, fix a booking flow that loses most of them, and write a user story an AI can build from.

Who is this for?

Every app is used by particular people in particular moments. Each of them is a user, and what they are trying to get done is their job. A feature only earns its place if it helps someone do their job.

YouBuild a plot booking app for the Hollow Lane community garden.
AI assistantDone! Signup has 12 fields, then a gardening quiz. There's a social feed, a harvest leaderboard and a dashboard with 9 tabs. Users will love it.
YouWhich users? Half our gardeners are parents on their phones, and some have had the same plot for years.
AI assistantGood point. I designed for a "typical user". Tell me who they really are.

Your task

  1. Meet all three neighbors: open each person's story.
  2. Send every feature to the person whose job it helps.
  3. Catch the three features nobody asked for.

Read the three stories first. The answers are in the details: the device, the time, the worry.

What you just did has a name: jobs to be done

People don't want a booking app. They "hire" it to make progress in their life. Maya is hiring a Saturday outside with her kids. That's why booking in a minute on her phone matters to her, and a harvest leaderboard doesn't. You can write any job in the same three parts:

When

the new season opens

I want to

keep the plot I've always had

So I can

carry on with the soil I've built up

Once you know the jobs, every feature has to answer one question: whose job does this help? When an AI proposes a feature and the honest answer is "users in general", it is usually padding. Each extra feature is more to build, test, explain and maintain.

Under the hood: where personas come from optional

Maya, Harold and Priya are personas: short, specific portraits of a kind of user. Good personas come from real conversations, support messages and usage data, not imagination.

An AI can draft personas in seconds, but treat them as guesses to check. Watching even a handful of real people try your app tends to reveal the biggest problems quickly.

Every step in a flow costs people

A flow is the path someone takes to finish a job, screen by screen. Every screen asks for a bit of time and effort, and at each one some people give up. Here's the booking flow the AI built. Imagine 1,000 neighbors open it this spring.

    Four ideas you just used

    Friction
    Anything that costs effort: a field, a decision, a code to find, a wall of text. Each bit loses a few people. Twelve fields lose a lot.
    Value before effort
    Show people what they get before you ask for work. Once Maya has picked her plot, making an account feels worth it.
    Defaults
    A sensible choice made in advance. Most people want this season, so pick it for them. Anyone can still change it.
    Error and empty states
    The screens for when something fails or there is nothing to show. A good one says what happened and offers a next step.
    Under the hood: how teams measure a real funnel optional

    Apps record an event each time someone reaches a step, like plot_selected or payment_succeeded. Counting those events per step gives a funnel, the chart you just played with. The share who reach the end is the conversion rate.

    Teams then change one step at a time and compare, often with an A/B test: half of visitors see the old version, half see the new one. The numbers in this lesson are illustrative, but the shape is typical: long forms and early account walls are common places for people to quit.

    Try it: write a user story

    Teams and AI tools build from short written stories. A user story says who wants what, and why. Acceptance criteria list what must be true for the story to count as done, so anyone, or any AI, can check the result.

    Your mission

    The AI wrote a story for Harold's renewal. It could describe any app for anyone. Rewrite it so it could only be about Harold, then press Run.

      A good story, taken apart

      Here is one for Maya. Yours is for Harold.

      As a parent booking on my phone, I want to reserve a plot near the playground, so that my kids can help without wandering off.

      WhoWants to doSo that
      Acceptance criteria
      • Plots within 20 meters of the playground are listed first.
      • Booking takes 3 screens or fewer on a phone.
      • If the last playground plot is taken, the app suggests the closest free plots. what can go wrong

      Notice what's missing: no buttons, colors or layouts. The story leaves the design open. The criteria say how you'll know it works.

      Under the hood: Given, When, Then optional

      Many teams write criteria in a fixed shape: Given Harold had plot 12 last season, When he renews before March 1, Then plot 12 is reserved for him.

      Test tools can turn lines like these into automated checks. Ask an AI to "write the acceptance criteria as Given, When, Then, and add a test for each" and you get a spec and its tests together.

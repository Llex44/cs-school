Vague feedback gets random results

An AI designer does exactly what your words say. When your words don't say what to change, it guesses, and its guesses are the most common patterns on the web.

Why "make it pop" can't work

"Pop", "modern", "clean" and "premium" describe a feeling, not a change. To the AI, a hundred different edits would all count as "more modern". So it picks one, usually a popular one, and often breaks something you liked. Precise feedback names the element, the change and, when it matters, the value: "Make the headline a serif, twice the body size."

Anatomy of a brief

Feedback fixes a design after the fact. A brief gets it right the first time: the design in your head, written down before the AI starts. Every part you leave out is a gap, and the AI fills every gap with its default.

Your task

  1. Switch off three parts of the brief and see what the AI fills each gap with.
  2. Pick what to take from each reference, and nothing that isn't yours to take.

A sketch beats a paragraph

Layout is the hardest thing to describe in words. "Put the image next to the text" has a dozen readings. A 30-second box sketch has one. Draw it on paper and attach a photo, or type it:

[ Slow Clay                       Classes  Studio ]

[ HEADLINE, big serif     ]   [                 ]
[ Sat 10 & 17 Oct   3 left ]   [  mug drawing    ]
[ Sat 7 & 14 Nov    6 left ]   [                 ]
[ ( Book a seat · $95 )   ]

On phones: headline, dates, button, then the drawing.

Wireframes show layout and priority. They say nothing about color or fonts, and that's the point: you settle the structure first.

References: say what to take

"Make it like this site" tells the AI to copy something, but not what. It might copy the colors when you loved the spacing, or lift a logo that isn't yours. Name the part you want from each reference.

Under the hood: why AI design drifts to the average optional

A language model writes the most likely continuation of what it has seen. For "a landing page", the most likely page is the one repeated across millions of templates and tutorials: a centered hero, a gradient, three feature cards, two buttons. That's not a lack of taste. It's what "likely" means.

Every specific instruction removes some of those likely options. A good brief doesn't make the AI smarter. It makes the average irrelevant, because you've already decided the things it would have averaged.

    Cheat sheet

    Turn a feeling into an instruction. Keep this next to your AI chat.

    Instead of…Say something like…Why it works
    "Make it pop""Make Book a seat the only filled button, terracotta #B84A33, right under the dates."Names the element, the change and the value.
    "Cleaner""Remove the three cards and the badge. Double the space between sections."Says what goes, and by how much.
    "More modern""Serif headline, about twice the body size, left-aligned. No gradients.""Modern" means something different to everyone.
    "More premium""One accent color, lots of cream space, no shadows or glow."Premium is made of specific choices.
    "I don't like it""The headline and the button compete. Headline first, then dates, then the button."Describes the problem, not a mood.
    "Like this site""From this site, take the date list. Not its colors or logo."A reference needs instructions.
    "Try again""Go back to version 3. Change only the headline."Anchors on what works; one change at a time.
    "Why does it look like that?""List every choice you made that wasn't in my brief."Turns hidden guesses into decisions.

    A brief you can copy

    Seven lines, each one a decision the AI no longer has to guess. Swap in your own project.

    Audience: adults who have never touched clay, booking on their phones.
Goal: book a seat in the next beginner class.
Content, most important first:
  1. Headline "Make your own mug in two Saturdays."
  2. The next two class dates, with seats left.
  3. $95, clay and firing included.
  4. One button: "Book a seat".
Layout: text on the left, mug illustration on the right.
  On phones: headline, dates, button, then the picture.
Reference: a ceramics magazine cover. Take its big serif
  headline and wide margins. Nothing else.
Constraints: terracotta #B84A33 on cream #FBF4EA.
  All text passes WCAG AA contrast (4.5 : 1).
Don'ts: no gradients, no emoji, no feature cards,
  don't center everything.
Before you start: list any choice you make that isn't in this brief.
